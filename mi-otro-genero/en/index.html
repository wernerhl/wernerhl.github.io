The book in brief

Question. Why did the employment gap between sisters and brothers within the same household in Bolivia fall steadily until the end of the twentieth century and almost stop falling in the twenty-first, just when young women overtook their brothers in years of schooling, expanded their rights and gained a decisive presence in public life?

Argument. Gender inequality begins in the social history of one's own time: who can count on the availability of others and who learns to organize their life around the care of others. By comparing sisters and brothers aged 17 to 30 raised under the same roof, the book distinguishes between the formation of gender norms (the learning and role models that shape aspirations) and their imposition (the domestic obligations, restrictions and relations of authority that sustain compliance). Until 2001, the employment gap between siblings narrowed as employment expanded in the mothers' generation and basic restrictions relaxed. After 2001, neither daughters' higher education nor maternal employment suffices to close the remaining difference (which in 2024 amounts to about ten more employed brothers for every hundred sisters and hundred brothers), because the reforms transformed the school, the laws and the state but left intact the assignment of care and time within the household.

About the book

To study, take a job, take part in public life or build a personal project, someone has to do the daily work that sustains existence. When a society takes for granted that women will be available to take on care, feeding and cleaning, it organizes the functioning of firms, public services and families around that expectation. This book investigates the persistence of that order in the face of deep transformations: today young sisters in Bolivia study more years than their brothers, have largely left housework as their exclusive activity and look for work as often as they do, but they do not gain access to employment in the same proportion.

The comparison between a sister and a brother raised in the same household offers a privileged angle from which to examine this brake. Two people can share housing, social origin and family resources, and yet grow up subject to opposite expectations: one is expected to seek income outside the home; the other is asked to remain available to provide care. To unravel how apparently reasonable family decisions reproduce cumulative disadvantages, the work analytically separates two mechanisms that feed each other: the formation of preferences through the models observed in the mother and the community, and the imposition of norms through the sharing of domestic burdens, the control of resources, family authority and the costs of departing from expected conduct.

With Bolivia as its central axis, in comparative dialogue with the historical and Andean trajectories of Peru and with the measurements of time use and family restrictions from Mexico, the study traverses half a century of domestic and institutional life. It examines the home, the school, couple formation, the labour market, violence and municipal politics to answer an underlying question: what material and institutional conditions allow a person to govern their own time within a society of interdependent beings.

Main findings

  1. Two stages in half a century: Measured within the household among children aged 17 to 30 (under the 19th ICLS definition of employment), the gap fell sharply until 2001 in parallel with the rise in employment in the mothers' generation, a pattern compatible with the intergenerational transmission of new norms and the easing of initial restrictions.
  2. The exhaustion of the traditional drivers: After 2001, maternal employment, women's labour force participation in the community and daughters' higher education kept growing, but they distinguish less and less the households where the gap between siblings is small.
  3. Equal search, unequal access: Young women left domestic work behind as their main activity; they exceed their brothers in schooling and look for work at the same rate, but face persistent barriers to actually obtaining employment.
  4. The internal redistribution of care: Part of the gap that persists in 2024 (ten more employed brothers for every hundred sisters and hundred brothers) lies in the intra-family assignment of time; in a minority of households, the mother's move into the labour market shifts the care of young children directly to a sister.
  5. The limit of institutional reforms: The reforms of recent decades transformed the state, the education system, criminal legislation and family law, but none acted on the distribution of care within the household, a terrain that official statistics in Bolivia do not yet record systematically in hours or in restrictions.

“Observing a sister and a brother makes it possible to place the capacity to govern one's own life within a shared family history. Following their trajectories leads to examining the social order that distributes between them the work of sustaining life and the possibilities of giving it a direction of their own.”

Data and reproducibility

The research combines half a century of census microdata, household surveys and administrative records in three countries:

  • Bolivia: 2024 Population and Housing Census (INE) and the censuses of 1976, 1992, 2001 and 2012 (10 per cent samples of IPUMS International); Household Survey and Demographic and Health Survey (EDSA); executed municipal budgets and electoral results.
  • Peru: National censuses of 1993, 2007 and 2017 (10 per cent samples of IPUMS International).
  • Mexico: 2020 Population and Housing Census, extended questionnaire (INEGI), together with the ENIGH and the ENDIREH to examine dimensions of unpaid work and family restrictions.
  • Regional harmonisation and open code: Harmonised surveys of SEDLAC. Except for figures published by other institutions, all calculations belong to the authors; the technical appendix documents in full the sources, the programs, the results registry and the coding decisions.

The authors

Werner L. Hernani-Limarino is a development economist at AI Analytics and the Bolivian Academy of Economic Sciences. His research covers poverty measurement, distributional analysis and intergenerational mobility in Bolivia and Latin America.

Website

Alejandra Villegas Gutierrez holds a doctorate in Critical Gender Studies from the Universidad Iberoamericana and is part of that university's Department of Economics. Her research areas are gender economics, economic development and financial inclusion.

Google Scholar

The series Desigualdad en Bolivia

Desigualdad en Bolivia brings together books on who gets what in Bolivia and why those differences persist, using the censuses from 1976 to 2024 and household surveys. Each volume examines one form of inequality:

  • the one that separates sisters and brothers raised in the same home, in Mi otro género (volume 1, this book);
  • the one passed from parents to children within each community, in El paisaje roto;
  • the one left by the distribution of the losses and gains of the crisis and the adjustment of 2019 to 2026, in La distribución de la crisis.
