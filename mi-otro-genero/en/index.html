Mi otro género

Sisters, brothers, and the formation and imposition of the norms that separate them

Werner L. Hernani-Limarino
Alejandra Villegas Gutierrez

First edition · 2026 · 296 pages · Written in Spanish · Free download

In Bolivia, young women now study more than their brothers, yet within the families where both still live they are employed less often, and that gap has barely fallen since 2001. This book asks why, and separates what a daughter has learned to want from what other people decide for her.

About the book

The gap is measured inside the household: between sisters and brothers aged 17 to 30 of the household head, under the definition of employment of the 19th International Conference of Labour Statisticians. In 2024 it amounts to about ten more employed brothers than sisters for every hundred of each. Half a century ago it was much larger.

To understand why it has barely fallen, the book compares sisters and brothers raised in the same household, using censuses and household surveys from Bolivia, Peru and Mexico. It examines the home, life as a couple, school, the labour market, violence and municipal councils.

What it finds

  1. The gap fell until 2001 along with the employment of women of the mother's generation. The pattern is compatible with formation passed from one generation to the next and with restrictions that were relaxing.
  2. After 2001 those conditions kept spreading, but they distinguish households with a small gap less and less. This holds for mothers' employment, for the employment of women in the community and for daughters' higher education.
  3. Sisters largely stopped reporting housework as their main activity. They study more than their brothers and look for work as often as they do, without reaching employment as often.
  4. Part of the remaining gap lies in how time and care are assigned inside the home. In a minority of households, the care of a young child passes to a sister when the mother goes to work. The data do not allow attributing the gap to a single mechanism.
  5. The reforms of the period reached the state, the school, criminal law and family law. None acted on the assignment of care within the household, an area that Bolivia does not yet measure in hours or in restrictions.

From the preface

The possibility of having one's own time has a social history. To study, take a job, take part in public life or build a personal project, we need someone to do the daily work that sustains our existence. The distribution of that work organizes the opportunities of each member of a family. It also establishes who can count on the availability of others and who learns to organize their life around their needs. In that relation between one's own time and obligations toward other people, a central part of gender inequality begins.

This book starts from a question about the persistence of that order amid deep changes. […]

The comparison between sisters and brothers makes it possible to approach that question from an especially revealing place. Two people can share a dwelling, family resources and a considerable part of their surroundings, and grow up subject to different expectations about what they should do with their lives. One may be asked to remain available to provide care. The other may be expected to seek income outside the home.

Two reasons: formation and imposition

Formation

The learning through which certain activities, aspirations and ways of living come to seem appropriate for a woman or for a man. A mother's experience, the paths of other women in the community and the possibilities visible in the surroundings can shape what a daughter finds desirable and attainable.

Imposition

The mechanisms that sustain compliance with those expectations: the assignment of domestic duties, restrictions on mobility, control of resources and sanctions for departing from expected conduct. Their force depends on who can demand and who is able to resist.

The two processes feed each other. The restrictions of one generation set the experiences within reach of the next, and new experiences can change what is considered possible and legitimate. The book poses questions about each mechanism and leaves their weight to empirical work.

Data and reproducibility

The book uses Bolivia's 2024 Population and Housing Census (INE) and Mexico's 2020 census, extended questionnaire (INEGI); the Bolivian censuses of 1976, 1992, 2001 and 2012 and the Peruvian censuses of 1993, 2007 and 2017, in the 10 per cent samples of IPUMS International (University of Minnesota); the Bolivian Household Survey and Demographic and Health Survey; Mexico's ENIGH and ENDIREH; the harmonised household surveys of SEDLAC; and executed municipal budgets and electoral results from Bolivia. Except for figures published by other institutions, all calculations are the authors' own. Appendix B describes the sources, the registry of results, the programs and the coding decisions.

The authors

Werner L. Hernani-Limarino is a development economist at AI Analytics and the Bolivian Academy of Economic Sciences. His research covers poverty measurement, distributional analysis and intergenerational mobility in Bolivia and Latin America.

Website · Working papers

Alejandra Villegas Gutierrez holds a doctorate in Critical Gender Studies from the Universidad Iberoamericana and is part of that university's Department of Economics. Her research areas are gender economics, economic development and financial inclusion.

The series Desigualdad en Bolivia

Desigualdad en Bolivia brings together books on who gets what in Bolivia and why those differences persist, using the censuses from 1976 to 2024 and household surveys. Each volume examines one form of inequality:

  • the one that separates sisters and brothers raised in the same home, in Mi otro género (volume 1, this book);
  • the one passed from parents to children within each community, in El paisaje roto;
  • the one left by the distribution of the losses and gains of the crisis and the adjustment of 2019 to 2026, in La distribución de la crisis.
